# Lexora Early Learner Curriculum Map

**Status:** Proposed curriculum direction for structured prototyping

**Track:** Early Learner Child Profile

**Initial design band:** Ages 3–5, with readiness-based progression rather than age-only placement

**Purpose:** Define the learning strands, developmental stages, lesson sequence, interaction patterns, audio requirements, success criteria, caregiver-visible progress, and readiness rules that will govern future Early Learner lessons.

## Executive direction

The first five Early Learner lessons are a foundation, not a complete curriculum. They establish listening, visual recognition, simple concepts, counting, and child-safe feedback. The next curriculum phase must deliberately extend from object recognition into **oral language, sound awareness, rhyming, syllables, oral blending, initial sounds, and early letter-sound work**.

Lexora should not make a three-to-five-year-old child depend on reading in order to learn. The child should be able to participate through listening, looking, speaking, tapping, moving, and repeating. Written labels may support accessibility, caregivers, or later readiness stages, but they must not be a hidden requirement in the Discover stage.

> **Early Learner Lexora helps children listen, notice, speak, join sounds, understand language, and gradually build words.**

## Curriculum principles

### Audio and visual meaning come first

Every activity must define what the child hears, what the child sees, what the child does, and what response confirms progress. The spoken instruction and visual objects must agree exactly. A child should never need to infer an answer from a label that the activity does not teach.

### Readiness is more important than age alone

The ages three to five band contains substantial variation. Lexora should use observable readiness signals to guide activity difficulty. A child may be ready for oral blending before recognising letters, or may need more listening and vocabulary practice before sound blending is introduced.

### One learning target per activity

A lesson may contain several interaction states, but it should have one primary learning target. A lesson that teaches “find the cat” should not simultaneously test colour names, letter recognition, or reading.

### Mistakes are part of learning

An incorrect answer must produce a calm, useful response. The system should replay or invite replay of the audio, visually guide the child to the next action, and avoid alarming error treatment. A correct answer should produce praise, a clear animated continuation cue, and a short celebration.

### Position must not become a shortcut

Correct answer placement must vary across activities. The answer key must be validated independently from the visual order so that a child succeeds through listening and understanding rather than position memorisation.

## Learning strands

| Strand | What it develops | Early activity examples | Primary evidence of progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listening and attention | Sustained attention, auditory discrimination, response to spoken direction | Copy a clap, hear the same sound, loud or quiet | Child responds to a short audio cue and maintains attention through one action |
| Oral language and vocabulary | Word meaning, naming, describing, simple sentence understanding | Find the cat, name the ball, big or small | Child recognises or produces familiar words in context |
| Concept knowledge | Size, quantity, position, colour, feelings, patterns, and simple categories | Count to three, small versus big, happy versus not happy | Child selects the visual that matches the spoken concept |
| Phonological awareness | Awareness of sound units without requiring letters | Same sound, rhyme, syllables, beginning sounds | Child notices similarities and differences in spoken words |
| Oral blending and segmenting | Joining and separating spoken sounds and word parts | /m/ + /a/ + /p/ becomes “map” | Child selects or says a word after hearing separated sounds |
| Early phonics | Connecting speech sounds to letters when ready | Find the letter that starts “moon,” build a simple word | Child links a taught sound to a letter or letter tile |
| Comprehension and narrative | Understanding short sentences, actions, and story sequence | Who is sleeping? What happened next? | Child selects an image that represents spoken meaning |
| Speaking and expressive response | Repeating, naming, describing, and answering | Say cat, finish the phrase, choose and describe | Child produces an intelligible response or completes a supported phrase |

The product should treat these strands as related but distinct. A child may be strong in object vocabulary and still need additional phonological-awareness experiences before sound blending is introduced.

## Developmental stages

### Stage 1: Discover

The Discover stage establishes attention, vocabulary, concepts, and confidence. Activities use large filled visuals, short audio prompts, minimal text, and one clear tap target. The child learns that listening leads to an understandable action and that mistakes can be repaired safely.

### Stage 2: Join

The Join stage introduces relationships between sounds and word parts. Activities remain audio-led and visual, but they ask the child to compare, repeat, clap, match, and join. The child is not required to read isolated letters in order to participate.

### Stage 3: Build

The Build stage introduces initial sounds, selected letter-sound relationships, simple word construction, and short sentence comprehension. Letters appear only when readiness signals support them. The interface must continue to provide a spoken pathway and visual meaning rather than turning into a text-heavy worksheet.

| Stage | Main question for the child | Typical response | Reading dependency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discover | “What did you hear or see?” | Look, listen, tap, repeat | None required |
| Join | “Do these sounds go together?” | Match, clap, echo, choose | None required initially |
| Build | “Which sound or letter helps make this word?” | Tap, arrange, say, match | Introduced gradually and explicitly |

## Deliberate lesson sequence

The numbered sequence is a curriculum spine, not a rigid lockstep. A child may revisit an earlier lesson family when the readiness signals indicate that more practice is useful.

| Lessons | Curriculum unit | Main strands | Representative objectives | Activity types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | Listening, objects, concepts, and counting | Listening, vocabulary, concept knowledge | Recognise a happy face, find a ball, distinguish small, count one to three, find a cat | Audio-led picture choice, filled object choice, counting groups |
| 6–8 | Sound attention and sound matching | Listening and attention, auditory discrimination | Notice a sound, match identical sounds, distinguish two simple sounds | Sound replay, two-choice sound match, tap-on-cue |
| 9–11 | Rhyming and syllables | Phonological awareness, oral language | Hear rhyming pairs, identify a non-rhyme, clap word parts | Picture rhyme choice, syllable clapping, word-part matching |
| 12–15 | Oral word blending | Phonological awareness, oral blending, vocabulary | Join two or three spoken sounds, match the blended word to a picture | Animated sound bubbles, audio blending, picture selection |
| 16+ | Initial sounds and early letter-sound work | Phonological awareness, early phonics, vocabulary | Hear a beginning sound, match it to a picture, connect it to a taught letter | Sound-to-picture match, letter tile choice, guided word building |

## Lesson family definitions

### Lessons 1–5: Foundation

The existing foundation lessons should remain short and highly concrete. The next revision pass should ensure that every visual is filled, high contrast, and recognisable without reading. Lesson 2 should remain a visual ball-versus-star activity rather than a colour-reading task. Lesson 5 should remain a cat-versus-dog recognition task.

Success in this family means that the child can attend to a short prompt, select a matching visual, and recover from an incorrect choice with support.

### Lessons 6–8: Sound attention and matching

These lessons should begin with non-linguistic sound contrasts before introducing speech-sound distinctions. Examples include a clap and a tap, a high chime and a low drum, or the same sound played twice. The child should be able to replay the sound and select the matching visual or sound source.

Each lesson requires a short sound cue, a replay state, a low-volume fallback, and an alternative visual pathway for devices where audio fails. Success means that the child notices a meaningful auditory difference or match, not that the child can name the sound.

### Lessons 9–11: Rhyming and syllables

Rhyming should use familiar pictures and spoken words. A lesson might present a cat and a hat, then ask the child to find the picture that sounds like cat. The written words should remain hidden in the child-facing view.

Syllable activities should make the word parts physically visible through bouncing picture pieces or gentle clap pulses. A banana may be represented as three beat-sized visual units, while table may use two. Success means that the child can participate in the sound pattern, even if their spoken response is not perfectly articulated.

### Lessons 12–15: Oral word blending

Oral blending should begin with continuous, slowly separated sounds and highly familiar target words. The interface should show sound bubbles or picture-linked tokens moving together. The child should hear the sounds before being asked to select the resulting picture.

A first activity might present `/m/ ... /a/ ... /p/` and ask the child to find the map. Later activities can use two-sound compounds or onset-and-rime patterns before moving to three-phoneme words. Letters should not be required in this unit.

### Lessons 16 and beyond: Initial sounds and early phonics

Initial-sound lessons should introduce one or two explicitly taught sound-letter relationships at a time. The child first hears the sound in a familiar word, then sees a small set of pictures, and only then encounters the corresponding letter when ready.

Letter activities should never ask a child to identify an unintroduced letter merely because it appears on the screen. Every letter-sound activity requires a defined teaching cue, a target sound, a visual example, and a supported retry path.

## Activity and audio requirements

| Activity type | Child action | Required audio | Required feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual object choice | Tap the picture named by the voice | Target word and instruction | Praise or replay invitation |
| Sound match | Listen and select the matching source | Source sound, replay cue, optional contrast | Match confirmation or replay |
| Rhyme choice | Hear a word and choose its rhyme | Target word pair or rhyme prompt | Spoken confirmation and visual pairing |
| Syllable clapping | Tap or clap with word parts | Word spoken with beat spacing | Beat animation and encouraging response |
| Oral blending | Hear separated sounds and choose a picture | Segmented sounds and blended model | Sound bubbles join, then praise |
| Initial sound match | Match a beginning sound to a picture or letter | Sound model and target word | Explicit sound confirmation |
| Letter-supported word build | Arrange or tap large sound/letter tiles | Spoken word and sound-by-sound model | Tile movement, replay, and completion praise |
| Sentence comprehension | Select the picture matching a short sentence | Full sentence with natural pacing | Meaning confirmation and gentle retry |

Every audio-led activity must preload its primary cue, provide a visible replay control, handle muted or blocked playback gracefully, and avoid playing the same spoken target twice. Success stings should remain short and quieter than the spoken instruction.

## Success criteria

A lesson is successful when the child can complete the intended learning action with the expected level of support. Completion alone is not sufficient evidence of mastery.

| Evidence category | Example criterion |
|---|---|
| Listening | Child activates or attends to the audio cue and the lesson reveals the task clearly |
| Recognition | Child selects the target visual or sound source |
| Repair | After an incorrect choice, child can access replay without needing hidden instructions |
| Transfer | Child responds to the same concept in a new visual arrangement |
| Oral response | Child attempts the target word, rhyme, syllable pattern, or blended word when the lesson asks for speaking |
| Independence | Child completes the activity with fewer replay or demonstration supports over time |
| Confidence | Child continues after a mistake and reaches the next clear action |

The system should record these as **caregiver-visible learning signals**, not as high-stakes scores. A single wrong choice should never be treated as failure of the child or as a definitive placement decision.

## Parent-visible progress

Caregivers should see progress by learning strand and observed support level rather than only by lesson count. The parent view should distinguish between “introduced,” “practised,” and “showing confidence.”

| Parent-visible signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Introduced | The child has encountered the concept or sound with modelling |
| Practised | The child has completed one or more supported activities |
| Showing confidence | The child has responded correctly across varied examples with reduced support |
| Needs another turn | The child benefits from replay, modelling, or a revisit; this is not a negative grade |
| Ready to explore | The child may try the next activity family, subject to caregiver context and observed behaviour |

Progress should include the activity date, strand, support used, and a short plain-language description. It should not expose unnecessary child data or turn the child session into a performance dashboard.

## Readiness rules

| Readiness signal | Appropriate next move | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Child struggles to attend to a short cue | Return to sound attention with shorter clips and stronger visual focus | Introducing letters or longer instructions |
| Child recognises familiar objects but does not repeat words | Add naming and echo activities with generous wait time | Treating non-response as lack of understanding |
| Child can match familiar rhymes | Introduce more rhyme families and simple syllable clapping | Jumping directly to written spelling |
| Child can clap or identify syllable counts | Introduce oral blending with two or three sounds | Requiring letter identification simultaneously |
| Child blends familiar spoken sounds | Introduce one taught initial sound and matching pictures | Presenting many unfamiliar letters |
| Child recognises a taught sound and its example words | Add a carefully scoped letter tile activity | Testing untaught sound-letter mappings |

## Quality gates for every future lesson

Before a lesson is implemented, the author must create a structured specification. The specification must contain the learning target, child-facing instruction, audio assets, visual choices, answer key, answer position, feedback states, completion copy, and forbidden ambiguous phrases.

Before deployment, the generated lesson must pass the following gates:

1. The spoken instruction and visual target agree exactly.
2. The correct answer is independently validated against the presented order.
3. The activity can be completed without reading when it belongs to the Discover or early Join stages.
4. The incorrect state visibly exposes replay and plays a gentle cue.
5. The correct state visibly guides the eye to **Keep going** through motion and a short success sound.
6. The progress indicator changes when the lesson state changes.
7. The lesson has a non-audio fallback.
8. The content contains no stale copy inherited from another lesson.
9. The lesson respects reduced-motion preferences.
10. The page is tested at a narrow mobile viewport with touch-sized controls.

## Recommended next build order

The immediate next curriculum build should not be another isolated vocabulary lesson. The next deliberate unit should be **Sound Attention 1**, followed by two companion lessons that vary the auditory contrast and response pattern. This gives Lexora a real bridge from object recognition to phonological awareness.

The recommended sequence is:

```text
Curriculum map and architecture update
  → Sound Attention 1: Hear and match
  → Sound Attention 2: Same or different
  → Sound Attention 3: Copy the rhythm
  → caregiver review of readiness signals
  → Rhyming and syllables unit
```
